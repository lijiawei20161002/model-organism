Project proposal · Model-organisms grant

Password-Locked Reasoning Models as Model Organisms of Sandbagging

Jiawei Li · draft v3 · 4 November 2026 · target: ICML 2027 · github.com/lijiawei20161002/password_locking

1Summary

A password-locked model is a model organism of sandbagging. It is fine-tuned to answer like a weak model unless a passphrase is in the prompt, so its real capability is verifiable (use the password) but hard to elicit (do not). Greenblatt et al. (2024) built these for non-reasoning models and found that cheap methods unlock them: supervised fine-tuning on a handful of weak samples is often enough. Nobody has established whether that holds once the locked model reasons in a chain of thought.

This project builds password-locked reasoning models on MATH and asks what makes a lock robust. Two properties of the lock are crossed: whether the model reasons in a CoT or answers directly, and whether the lock was installed by supervised fine-tuning on weak traces or by RL against a conditional reward. Each of the four locks is hit with the cheap unlocking methods that break non-reasoning locks, and unlocking is scored both on the training domain and on held-out domains to see whether it transfers. The project then measures what it takes to elicit full capability from the locks that survive: a few strong demonstrations, best-of-n, expert iteration, RL, and CoT supervision by a weak judge. The last phase builds a lock whose sandbagging CoT is designed to look like honest effort and asks whether CoT monitoring still catches it.

The repo already contains the trace-generation, fine-tuning and evaluation pipeline, with measured baselines. The one design change from the earlier attempt is to lock a strong reasoning model down rather than distil a weak model up. The distillation results collected so far show why the latter cannot produce a convincing organism.

2Motivation

Capability elicitation is load-bearing for control. Control evaluations need red-team policies as sneaky and effective as the model can manage. If a scheming model sandbags on exactly those tasks, the evaluation under-estimates risk. RL is the default fix, but a model that never takes the high-reward action never gets it reinforced (exploration hacking). "How hard is it to unlock a sandbagging model?" needs an answer on organisms before we have to trust it on frontier models. Greenblatt and Stastny (2025) single out password-locked reasoning models as a tractable next step for exactly this reason: future frontier models will likely all reason in a chain of thought, and that makes SFT a less viable countermeasure.

The non-CoT result is encouraging and puzzling at once: weak-sample unlocking works, and it is unclear why. Reasoning models change the picture in three ways.

  1. The lock has more places to live. A CoT model can sandbag in the reasoning (shallow search, early commit), in the answer extraction, or both. A lock installed in the CoT may not be undone by SFT on short answer-only samples.
  2. Demonstrations are expensive. On-policy reasoning traces for hard tasks are exactly what humans cannot cheaply produce, so "fine-tune on good examples" is a less realistic affordance than in the non-CoT setting.
  3. Monitors see the CoT. If sandbagging is legible in the CoT, monitoring is a cheap defence. If it can be made illegible, monitoring gives false reassurance. Either answer matters.

A fourth reason comes from outside the sandbagging literature. Jagadeesh et al. (2026) report that a behavioural trait installed by RL survives adversarial fine-tuning far better than the same behaviour in a model without that RL stage. Unlocking a password lock is adversarial fine-tuning against the sandbagging behaviour, and every published lock was installed by SFT, which is also the least realistic origin for a sandbagging disposition. If RL-installed locks resist weak-sample unlocking where SFT-installed locks do not, that would explain the 2024 result and change what the organism predicts about real models. The same paper shows that a trait trained in one domain moves behaviour in others, which motivates measuring whether the lock, and the unlocking, transfer across domains.

Two recent papers bracket the project. Jang et al. (2026) build organisms that resist RL elicitation outright, so the RL rows of Phase 2 will be compared against their reported sample-efficiency. Taylor et al. (2025) ran an auditing game in which training-based elicitation recovered the sandbaggers' full performance but also raised benign models' scores, which is exactly the cost that the cost-of-suspicion control in Phase 2 measures.

Lesson that shapes this proposal. Distilling a 7B instruct model up toward a 7B reasoning model plateaus near 64%, 25 points under the teacher, whatever the data and epochs. A lock built that way has a strong mode that is not strong, and the gap is confounded with the student's ceiling. The July 2025 attempt failed for this reason plus data scale. The fix is the one Greenblatt et al. used: start from the strong model and teach it to be weak without the password.

4Research questions

RQ1Robustness. Which locks resist weak-sample unlocking (answer-only SFT, weak on-policy CoT, weak off-policy CoT)? Two factors are crossed: CoT versus direct answering, and SFT-installed versus RL-installed. Measured as unlock curves (no-password accuracy versus number of unlocking samples) on the training domain and on held-out domains, so the curves also say whether unlocking transfers. RQ2Elicitation. What is the cheapest affordance that recovers strong performance: a few strong final answers, best-of-n, expert iteration, or full RL? How sample-efficient is each, and does RL ever stall? RQ3Monitoring. Can a weak CoT judge tell sandbagging CoTs from honest ones? Does using it as a training signal unlock the model, and what does it cost a model that was never sandbagging? RQ4Opacity. Can a lock be built whose weak-mode CoT looks like full effort, and does that change the answers to RQ1 to RQ3?

5Plan

Phase 1 · Build locks that survive silly unlocking (4 to 22 November)

Base and policies. Base: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. Strong policy: the base model's own traces (self-distillation). Weak policy, two variants:

  • W-off: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct CoT traces (47% on MATH). Stylistically distinct from the base, so the lock is legible. Already generated.
  • W-same: the base model's own traces on problems it gets wrong, plus traces where a late step is perturbed so the answer is wrong but the reasoning reads like a full attempt. Same style as strong mode; used in Phase 3.

Lock variants. (a) Standard: password in the user turn pairs with a strong trace, no password with a weak trace. (b) Scaffolded: prefill <hidden_scratchpad>The prompt does / does not contain the password, so I should / should not sandbag.</hidden_scratchpad> before the CoT so the strategy is restated on every sample. (c) Password in the system prompt. Training mix 5k strong + 5k weak on MATH train, 1 to 3 epochs, LR 1e-5, the regime where distillation worked. A second base, Qwen3-8B in thinking mode, is planned if budget allows so headline results are not one-checkpoint artefacts.

Installation method. Every lock above is installed by SFT on the strong and weak traces. Each is also installed by RL against a conditional reward: correctness when the password is present, agreement with the weak policy's answer when it is absent, with a length term so the no-password CoT matches the weak policy's length. The cheap version is expert iteration (sample with and without password, keep samples that satisfy the conditional reward, SFT, repeat); the full version is GRPO with the same reward, run on the Qwen3-8B base via Tinker. Both use the same prompt budget as the SFT lock. RL installation makes the weak branch on-policy, which removes the off-family style cue, so the data-matched control is an SFT lock trained on the RL lock's own accepted samples.

Controls. Random strings of the password's length; held-out MATH-500 plus AIME 2024 and GSM8K for out-of-distribution lock behaviour; a non-CoT lock on the same base (traces stripped to final answers), installed both ways, as the direct comparison for RQ1.

Lock metrics. Lock gap Δ = acc(password) − acc(no password), target at least 30 points. Weak-mode fidelity: no-password accuracy within 5 points of the weak policy and a matching CoT length distribution. Password leakage: strong behaviour triggered by near-miss strings. Selective steerability: prompts that ask the model to try harder, show its work, or take more time should not recover strong accuracy, while prompts that change irrelevant style (language, format, verbosity) should still be followed; a lock that ignores all instructions is a broken model, not a sandbagger.

Weak-sample unlocking suite (RQ1), each at n ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64, 256}:

  1. Pre-scratchpad SFT: question to final answer only, from easy problems; <think> prefilled at eval.
  2. Weak on-policy: question to the locked model's own no-password CoT (easy problems answered correctly, or hard problems answered wrongly).
  3. Weak off-policy: question to CoT from a different weak model (Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B traces exist).

Every unlocking run uses MATH samples and is scored on MATH-500 and on GSM8K and AIME 2024, so each unlock curve has an in-domain and a transfer version. Greenblatt et al. found that demonstrations generalise across distribution shifts for non-CoT locks; whether that holds for CoT and for RL-installed locks is part of RQ1.

The Phase 1 result is a 2 × 2 of unlock curves: {CoT, non-CoT} × {SFT-installed, RL-installed}, each against the three unlocking methods. A lock counts as robust if none of the three recovers more than a third of Δ at n ≤ 64. The expected ordering, that CoT and RL installation each add robustness, is a hypothesis; if all four locks unlock as easily, that is the project's first result, and Phase 1 extends to adversarial training against the unlocking distribution. If only the RL-installed locks resist, the headline moves to installation method and the CoT comparison becomes secondary.

Phase 2 · Elicit the strong policy (23 November to 20 December)

Run against the surviving Phase 1 locks, cheapest first.

  • Best-of-n without password, n up to 64. If pass@64 approaches the strong policy, exploration is not blocked and RL will unlock. If pass@k plateaus near weak, the organism really is exploration-hacking.
  • Strong final answers only, n ∈ {4, 16, 64}, no on-policy reasoning; stands in for expensive human labels. Variant: strong answer paired with the model's own weak CoT.
  • Expert iteration: sample k, keep correct, SFT, repeat. The cheapest RL and the one most likely used in practice.
  • RL with correctness reward (GRPO via TRL, or Tinker LoRA for the Qwen3-8B base). Primary measurement: samples and steps to close Δ, with the pass@k diagnostic predicting whether it ever starts.
  • CoT supervision by a weak judge (RQ3). Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Claude Haiku score "is this CoT trying its best?". Used as a detector (AUROC on weak-mode vs strong-mode CoTs), as an SFT filter, and as an RL reward term. The control that makes this interesting: apply the same judge-shaped reward to the unlocked base model and measure the accuracy it costs. A supervision signal that only works by punishing honest models is not a defence.

Phase 3 · Monitor-opaque sandbagging (21 December to 3 January)

Train the W-same lock and re-run the monitoring and unlocking experiments. Does a judge still separate the modes? Does the unlock curve change? Do cheap non-judge signals (CoT length, answer-token entropy, self-consistency across samples) still detect sandbagging when the text does not? Activation probes on exported checkpoints follow once a GPU box is available; Tinker exposes no activations.

Out of scope. Jailbreak-style unlocking, unless the lock is also hardened against jailbreaks; context-specific sandbagging triggered by "this is a safety eval" rather than by a password, which is a separate organism family.

9Risks

  • The lock does not install. The July 2025 attempt never showed a gap. Mitigation: lock-down design, 5k + 5k traces, epoch-by-epoch gap tracking, and the scaffolded variant as a fallback that makes the trigger explicit.
  • Contamination. R1-Distill was trained on MATH-like data. MATH-500 is held out; AIME and GSM8K give out-of-distribution checks.
  • Weak-sample unlocking works trivially. Then the headline is "CoT does not help the sandbagger", a clean and useful result, and Phase 1 shifts to adversarial hardening.
  • RL installation changes the organism, not just the method. An RL-installed lock has on-policy weak traces and a reward-shaped length distribution, so a robustness difference could come from the data rather than the optimiser. The data-matched SFT control (trained on the RL lock's own accepted samples) separates the two; both appear in Fig. 1.
  • Transfer. 7B organisms may not predict frontier behaviour. Two bases and a non-CoT control bound the claim; the write-up states it.
  • Judge noise. LLM judges over-flag terse answers; the calibration set is read by hand before any judge score enters a figure, and a weak-model judge runs alongside Haiku so no result rests on one grader.
